MEDELLÍN
How Gentrification
Is Changing My City

Laura Yissel Leaño Fajardo

Medellín is known as "the city of the eternal spring." But in the last few years, something has been changing — and not everyone is happy about it.

Colombia 5 Topics 2025 – 2026
Introduction

Why Medellín?

I chose Medellín because it's my country's most famous example of urban transformation. The city has changed a lot since the 90s — it's safer, greener and more modern than before.

But all this progress has a darker side. Rents are getting higher, foreigners are buying more property, and many local families are being pushed to the edges of the city. This process is called gentrification.

Note: Even though this was a team activity, I did it on my own.

What is gentrification?

It's when a poor or working-class neighborhood becomes more attractive to richer people. Prices go up, and original residents often can't afford to stay.

My approach

I looked at five aspects of the city: economy, environment, culture, education and health. All of them connect to the same problem: who gets to live in the city?

💼01

Economy.

The city is getting richer, but living here is getting harder.

Good news first

Medellín has become popular with digital nomads and foreign investors. Real estate, tourism and restaurants have grown. New buildings are going to keep appearing, especially in El Poblado.

But here's the problem

Rents are getting more and more expensive. One out of four new apartments is bought by a foreigner. The price per square meter has grown almost 17% between 2024 and 2025 — way faster than salaries.

Interesting fact

Airbnb hosts in Medellín made around US$133 million in 2025. The city now has more Airbnb listings per person than Madrid or Athens.

My opinion

The city is richer than before, but less affordable for the people born here. If the government doesn't regulate short-term rentals soon, things are going to get worse.

🌿02

Environment.

Green corridors — but greener for whom?

An amazing project

The city has planted thirty green corridors along roads and rivers. They've helped cool down some areas by up to 10 degrees. The project has also created over 700 jobs.

The unexpected effect

When a neighborhood becomes greener and prettier, property values go up — and long-time residents may not be able to afford living there. This is called "green gentrification."

🎭03

Culture.

When a city becomes prettier for tourists, it can feel less like home for locals.

Our roots

The Feria de las Flores has been going on since 1957. Places like Plaza Botero and Museo de Antioquia show how much the city cares about art.

The loss

In El Poblado and Manila, old houses are being torn down to build modern hotels. Someone even said that in some streets of Manila, only English is spoken now.

The resistance

In Comuna 13, hip-hop artists use music to talk about these problems. As Alcolirykoz raps: "The sound of gentrification is silence."

🎓04

Education.

The silent exodus of students to the outskirts.

A real priority

Education has always mattered a lot in Medellín. Programs like "Medellín, la más educada" helped the city change its image.

The new challenge

As rents get more expensive, poorer families are being pushed to the outskirts. In some areas, high school enrollment has fallen by more than 11%.

🩺05

Health.

Gentrification hurts more than your wallet — it also affects your mind.

More than hospitals

Medellín has a good healthcare system with modern hospitals. But health is also about feeling safe and at home in your neighborhood.

The hidden wound

Families forced to leave their neighborhoods often feel depressed, anxious and tired. They don't trust institutions anymore.

Conclusion

A price of progress — or a choice?

After researching all these aspects, I don't think gentrification is simply good or bad. It's more complicated than that.

The risk

If nothing changes, Medellín will become more unequal, less authentic and less welcoming for the people who built it.

The opportunity

With inclusive policies — like the Green Corridors — the city can grow and protect its people at the same time.
